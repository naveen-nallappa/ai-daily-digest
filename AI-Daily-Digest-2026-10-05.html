Major AI Updates
Schneider Electric (press release)

Schneider Electric agrees to buy PTC for $22.6 billion to build an industrial-AI franchise

Schneider Electric agreed on October 5 to buy US engineering-software maker PTC for $205 a share in cash, a 42.3% premium, valuing PTC at $22.6 billion in equity and $23.7 billion including debt. It is Schneider’s largest deal and follows its $3.1 billion purchase of industrial-AI firm Cognite in July. Chief executive Olivier Blum said the combination creates a “digital thread for the next generation of industrial AI.” Closing is expected by the third quarter of 2027.

Why it matters: Industrial buyers are paying software multiples for the data layer that physical AI runs on, a theme beyond chips and data centers. PMs should revisit valuations of remaining independent design and industrial-software names such as Autodesk, Bentley and Dassault, and income-focused clients holding Schneider should expect added deal leverage until the 2027 close.
Huawei (press release)

Qualcomm licenses Huawei AI and 5G patents and buys some of its US patents

Huawei and Qualcomm announced a multi-year cross-license on October 5 covering 5G, compute, AI and networking, the first between them to include 5G. Qualcomm will also buy certain Huawei US patents in compute, AI and networking, subject to regulatory approval. Bloomberg reported the licensed technology includes Huawei’s “LogicFolding” chip design. Huawei said the deal lifts its cumulative licensing agreements past $6.9 billion once it closes. Financial terms were not disclosed.

Why it matters: A US chip leader paying for Huawei’s AI and compute patents shows China’s chip know-how now carries licensing value despite export controls. PMs holding Qualcomm or semiconductor ETFs should watch for US regulatory review of the patent purchase, and treat Chinese chip IP as a factor in competitive analysis, not only a sanctions headline.
DigiTimes

Musk confirms TSMC talks on Terafab, putting Intel’s 14A role at risk

Elon Musk confirmed on October 3 that TSMC is in talks to join Terafab, his Texas chip plant: “Just discussions, but something may come of it.” Two structures are under discussion: TSMC owns and runs the fab with SpaceX committing to buy volume, or SpaceX holds a majority with TSMC supplying process technology. Terafab would supply only Tesla, SpaceX and xAI. Intel currently supplies its 14A process, and TSMC has not commented.

Why it matters: Intel’s foundry recovery case leans on landing anchor customers for 14A, and Terafab is one of the few named. PMs holding Intel on a turnaround thesis should treat a TSMC deal as a direct risk to that story, while TSMC holders gain another sign of US capacity demand beyond Apple and Nvidia.
BleepingComputer

OpenAI will test visual ads in ChatGPT image generation later in October

OpenAI said on October 5 it will begin testing visual ads shown while ChatGPT generates images, starting in the US later this month with a first group of advertisers. Ads will be labeled and kept separate from the generated image. Hightouch, Tealium and LiveRamp handle conversion measurement, and DoubleVerify and Integral Ad Science handle brand safety. OpenAI cites 1.2 billion weekly ChatGPT users and says ads do not influence answers.

Why it matters: Advertising is becoming a second revenue line for OpenAI ahead of its IPO, which puts it in competition with Google and Meta for brand budgets. PMs should track the ad tests as a signal for OpenAI’s valuation, and compliance teams should note that firm or product mentions in ChatGPT may increasingly sit beside paid placements.
AI in Wealth Management
Morningstar (press release)

Morningstar study finds advisors not using AI fell to 20% but time savings lag

Morningstar’s 2026 Investor Perspectives Advisor Study, released October 5, surveyed 501 US advisors between July 21 and August 16. The share not using AI fell to 20% from 33% in 2025, and 48% said AI significantly or moderately improved their workflow. Yet 56% still call administrative work their biggest barrier, and advisors spend 53% of their week with clients against a desired 63%. Only 18% rate AI tools highly reliable for investment decisions.

Why it matters: Adoption is near universal, but the promised four hours a week of client time has not arrived. Practice leaders should measure AI by hours moved to client work, not tools licensed, and target meeting notes and paperwork first, where 57% see value. Investment decisions remain a weak use case, so keep human sign-off on recommendations.
Financial Planning

Fidelity will drop RIAs under $100 million by June 2027, a week after backing Savvy

Fidelity told RIAs on October 1 that firms with less than $100 million in custody must reach that level or leave by June 30, 2027, the first time it has applied the minimum to existing clients. Financial Planning put the potentially affected pool at more than 20,500 state-registered advisors. RIABiz reported on October 2 that Fidelity’s backing of Savvy Wealth’s AI-native custodial platform, announced September 23, may absorb some of them. Michael Kitces called the move “bizarre.”

Why it matters: Fidelity is keeping large RIAs direct and pushing small ones toward an AI-native intermediary, a split other custodians may copy. Sub-$100 million firms should start custodian searches now, comparing Schwab, Altruist under Vanguard and Savvy on pricing and AI tooling, and check that client repapering and data portability fit the June 2027 deadline.
Pravar AI (press release)

Pravar AI launches an advisor agent that flags liquidity events and attrition risk

Pravar AI launched Pravar Wealth OS and its first product, Pravar for Advisors, on September 30, available now to RIAs and wealth firms in the US and India. The system reads client accounts, holdings and activity to flag upcoming liquidity events, assets held at other firms, early attrition signs and heirs who have never met the firm, then drafts next steps. A governance layer screens personal data and routes weak outputs to human review. No customers or funding were disclosed.

Why it matters: Next-best-action engines are moving from wirehouse builds to off-the-shelf tools for RIAs, aimed squarely at retention during the wealth transfer. Firms evaluating one should test it on their own departed-client history to see whether it would have flagged those exits, and confirm how client data moves between the US and India.
AI in Asset Management
Bloomberg

Firmus reserves half of its $5.5 billion AI data-center IPO for Nvidia and other backers

Australian AI data-center operator Firmus plans to allocate about half of its IPO of up to $5.5 billion to existing investors including Nvidia, Coatue, Blackstone and Jane Street, Bloomberg and Reuters reported on October 5. Priced at A$11 a share, the deal values Firmus at about $30.6 billion, nearly triple its August round. The institutional book opens October 6 and closes early on Thursday; ASX trading starts October 23. BofA, JPMorgan, Morgan Stanley and Morgans lead.

Why it matters: With half the deal pre-placed, the free float is thin, which can lift early trading but leaves new buyers exposed if insiders sell later. PMs considering an allocation should check lock-up terms in the October 12 prospectus, and weigh that Hong Kong’s recent AI listings mostly trade below their offer prices.
CME Group (press release)

CME sets October 5 debut for the first regulated GPU rental futures

CME Group and Silicon Data scheduled two NYMEX contracts to launch on October 5, pending regulatory review: Silicon Data H100 and B200 Rental Index Futures. Each contract covers one month of GPU rental cost, tracking Silicon Data’s hourly price indexes. Silicon Data is backed by trading firm DRW. CME’s Pete Keavey called compute “the currency of the AI age,” comparing it to the early oil derivatives market.

Why it matters: A listed price for GPU time gives lenders and funds a way to hedge and mark the chip collateral behind AI data-center credit. Private-credit allocators should ask managers whether they will use these contracts to value GPU-backed loans, and quants should watch early volume and the spread between H100 and B200 as a read on chip obsolescence.
Hedgeweek

Bridgewater’s machine-learning AIA Macro fund gains 16.4% this year on $4.5 billion

Bridgewater’s AIA Macro fund, run by machine learning since its late-2023 launch, returned 16.4% through September 29 and manages more than $4.5 billion, Reuters reported, citing people familiar with the results; Hedgeweek followed on October 2. The fund has returned 13.2% a year since inception. The flagship Pure Alpha gained 18.4% in the first nine months, despite a July sell-off in AI stocks that forced some managers to unwind technology positions.

Why it matters: Nearly three years of double-digit returns gives the largest machine-learning macro fund a live record allocators can underwrite. Institutional allocators should ask how AIA’s returns correlate with Pure Alpha before treating it as diversification, and request drawdown data from July, when AI-linked positioning hurt many discretionary managers.
