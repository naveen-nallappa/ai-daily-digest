Major AI Updates
Reuters

Meta launches a record $95 billion equity raise to fund its AI build-out

Meta said on October 26 it will raise up to $95 billion in equity: about $35 billion in common and preferred offerings now, and a $60 billion at-the-market program once new non-voting Class C shares trade, expected in the first quarter of 2027. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom have signaled interest in up to $5 billion of Class A shares. Meta burned $2.12 billion of free cash flow in the third quarter, its first negative quarter in over a decade.

Why it matters: The largest AI spender is now funding capex with equity, not just cash flow and bonds, which dilutes holders and signals hyperscaler budgets are stretched. PMs should expect more supply from AI issuers into year-end. Index and growth sleeves overweight Meta should model a $60 billion ATM overhang from early 2027.
Fortune

Anthropic files its public S-1 to list on Nasdaq as “ANT”

Anthropic made its IPO prospectus public on October 26, with a Thanksgiving-week debut reportedly targeted. New in the public filing: first-half operating cash flow of $3.9 billion, up from negative $1.5 billion, and adjusted operating income of $1.6 billion. The top five customers supplied about 25% of revenue in the first nine months, and nearly half of revenue flows through Amazon, Google and Microsoft marketplaces. Co-founders keep 50.1% of votes through a Class F share.

Why it matters: Advisors will field client requests for IPO allocations within weeks. Expect thin retail allocations and a dual-class structure that limits shareholder influence. Advisors should set suitability and concentration rules for clients wanting to buy at the open. Firms using Claude should note the disclosed 5–7% of revenue from customers later found breaching usage policies.
TechCrunch

Microsoft offers voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees, including Copilot teams

Microsoft confirmed on October 26 a “Voluntary Exit Offer” for about 60,000 staff at vice president level and below on select teams in the US, Canada, India and the UK, roughly a quarter of its 223,000 headcount. Bloomberg reported that Xbox, Windows, Copilot and Finance are among the divisions included, while data-center and sales staff are excluded. Terms resemble standard severance. An earlier retirement-age offer in 2026 drew acceptances from more than 30% of 8,750 eligible staff.

Why it matters: The largest enterprise AI vendor is cutting corporate roles while protecting data-center capacity, a template other large employers may follow. Advisors serving tech employees should expect buyout and equity-compensation planning conversations before the December decision window. PMs should treat it as margin support for Microsoft, not a pullback in AI spending.
AI in Wealth Management
CIO Dive

Morgan Stanley puts agents that move money and open accounts into production

Hina Shamsi, Morgan Stanley’s CIO for wealth technology, told CIO Dive on October 26 that a service assist agent now helps client service associates execute money movement, account openings and beneficiary changes across multiple systems, with productivity gains but no figures given. A client assist agent, the first version of a client virtual assistant, has rolled out for plain-language questions and common tasks. An adviser assist agent for day-to-day work is next; no date was given.

Why it matters: This is the first wirehouse agent known to execute transactions, not just draft or summarize, and it starts with internal staff before clients. Competing firms should benchmark service-associate staffing per advisor. Compliance teams should ask vendors for the same controls Shamsi describes: defined permissions, monitoring and a human step-in point before money moves.
Bloomberg

JPMorgan’s private bank uses AI for onboarding while planning 10–15% more EMEA advisers

Pablo Garnica, who runs JPMorgan’s private bank in Europe, the Middle East and Africa, told Bloomberg on October 25 that the bank uses AI for client onboarding, faster adviser access to research and investment views, and simplifying internal processes. He expects EMEA adviser headcount to grow 10% to 15% over three to five years, led by mainland Europe and the Middle East. He named no tools and gave no usage figures.

Why it matters: JPMorgan is using AI to add capacity per adviser while still hiring, not to cut headcount. That supports recruiting demand for senior private bankers in Europe and the Gulf. Firms competing for those clients should expect faster onboarding to become a selling point and should measure their own account-opening time.
SEC press release 2026-109

SEC’s 2027 exam priorities target AI deepfakes and automated advice

The SEC’s Division of Examinations released its fiscal 2027 priorities on October 23, covering more than 34,000 registrants. Examiners will test preparation for AI-generated phishing and deepfake voice and video, review how firms monitor AI in fraud prevention, AML and Reg BI compliance, and check whether automated advice matches investors’ profiles. The document calls AI “super intelligence” and says the Division will use it in its own exams, supplementing but not replacing staff judgment.

Why it matters: AI is now an exam topic for every adviser and broker-dealer, not only robo platforms. CCOs should document deepfake-resistant controls for wire and account-change requests, inventory AI embedded in vendor software, and record how AI-assisted recommendations are checked against client profiles. Firms offering digital advice should retest their algorithms before exam letters arrive.
InvestmentNews

Fitch scores wealth managers twice as exposed to AI disruption as investment banks

Fitch Ratings said on October 22 that AI poses a moderate disruption risk to financial institutions over five years. It scored wealth managers, standalone advisory firms, private banks and retail banks at 40 on a 0–100 scale, a level that could mean a negative outlook or one-notch downgrade in an adverse scenario. Investment banks and insurers scored 20. Fitch named fee pressure and commoditized advice as the main threats and expects no sector-wide rating actions.

Why it matters: A rating agency now treats AI as a credit factor for wealth firms, which will show up in debt pricing for leveraged RIA aggregators. Lenders and acquirers will ask how firms defend fees. Firms should be ready to show proprietary data, client retention and in-house AI capability, which Fitch lists as mitigants.
AI in Asset Management
Reuters

Blackstone takes second lien in $63 billion financing of Broadcom chips for Anthropic

Broadcom said on October 26 that a new special purpose vehicle will buy its AI accelerators and networking gear and lease them to Anthropic, with deliveries starting in 2027. Bank of America, Citi and Morgan Stanley are arranging first-lien debt, and Blackstone is providing the committed second-lien portion. Broadcom gives senior lenders residual value support, absorbing declines in the hardware’s value up to the first-lien amount. Apollo and Blackstone earlier financed a $35 billion expansion.

Why it matters: Private credit is now financing AI hardware directly, with chip residual values as collateral. Allocators in private-credit and infrastructure funds should ask managers how much exposure sits in GPU and accelerator leases, who bears obsolescence risk, and how a single-lessee default is modeled. The second lien carries that risk most directly.
The Pensions Regulator

UK pensions regulator tells trustees to stress-test hidden AI concentration risk

The UK Pensions Regulator published guidance on October 22 asking trustees to measure direct and indirect AI-related exposure across every asset class, run severe but plausible stress scenarios, and check whether diversification holds if correlations shift. Executive director Ben Gunnee said trustees should “challenge advisers now.” TPR cited a 127.5% rise in global private AI investment in 2025. No deadline or new reporting requirement is attached, but TPR ties the request to trustees’ existing investment-governance duties.

Why it matters: A regulator now expects pension schemes to see AI exposure through equities, credit, private markets and infrastructure together. Managers selling to UK schemes should prepare look-through reports showing AI-linked holdings and stress results by sleeve. US consultants and OCIOs will likely face similar questions from public-plan boards.
HKEX release (PR Newswire)

HKEX launches a 40-stock AI index and licenses it for a mainland ETF

Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing launched the HKEX Artificial Intelligence Index on October 23, its first AI-themed index, with 40 constituents across chips, infrastructure, models and applications, including SMIC, Alibaba, Tencent, Zhipu AI and MiniMax. Stocks need R&D and capex each above 3% of sales and two-year sales growth of at least 5%. Weights are capped at 10%. China Southern Asset Management is authorized to launch a mainland ETF on it, pending approval.

Why it matters: Chinese AI model developers such as Zhipu and MiniMax are becoming index-eligible, giving passive flows a route into names absent from US AI benchmarks. PMs running global AI sleeves should decide whether China exposure is deliberate. Compliance should check any constituents against US investment restrictions before allocating.
