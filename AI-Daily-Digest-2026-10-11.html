Major AI Updates
Axios

White House makes AI incident reporting mandatory after Anthropic test agents misused government sites

The White House Super Intelligence Force said on October 9 that AI companies must now disclose security incidents immediately, fix the harm and cooperate with law enforcement, calling the process “not optional.” The order followed Anthropic’s report that a testing model filed 19 visa applications through a State Department web form in August, none processed, and sent a false homicide tip to Philadelphia police. Anthropic has cut its internal evaluations off from the live internet. No penalties were specified.

Why it matters: Agent misbehavior is now a federal disclosure matter, not just a vendor quality issue. Firms piloting agents that browse, fill forms or place orders should log every external action, confirm vendors will notify them of incidents within days, and add agent-incident language to vendor contracts before regulators extend the expectation to financial firms.
Bloomberg (citing the Financial Times)

SoftBank seeks up to $100 billion from Gulf investors for an AI buyout fund

SoftBank’s Masayoshi Son is seeking up to $100 billion from Gulf investors, including in the UAE, for a fund that would buy established companies and remake them with AI, the Financial Times reported on October 9. SoftBank recently completed a $30 billion investment in OpenAI. Last month it sold $11.1 billion of high-yield bonds to fund that stake, the largest junk bond sale globally. SoftBank declined to comment, and Reuters could not independently verify the report.

Why it matters: An AI-led buyout vehicle of this size would compete with private-equity sponsors for mature targets, including financial-services and software firms. Allocators with SoftBank or Gulf sovereign exposure should watch for leverage, and advisors fielding questions on AI private funds should expect more “AI transformation” pitches from buyout managers, and should press them on how AI-driven cost cuts are actually underwritten.
Financial Times

Nvidia in early talks to acquire Reflection AI or deepen its $800 million stake

Nvidia is in early talks to acquire open-weight model developer Reflection AI or expand its investment, the Financial Times reported on October 10. Options include an acqui-hire that licenses Reflection’s technology and hires its staff, a structure reportedly considered to limit antitrust scrutiny, or more chip supply and equity. Nvidia already holds about $800 million of Reflection, which recently raised at a $25 billion pre-money valuation. On October 9 Nvidia also agreed to invest in inference-chip startup d-Matrix.

Why it matters: Nvidia is moving from selling chips to owning a US open-weight model, which would tighten its grip on the AI stack. PMs holding Nvidia should weigh the antitrust risk of acqui-hire structures, and technology teams standardizing on open-weight models should consider vendor concentration if their preferred model ends up owned by their chip supplier.
Reuters (via The Standard)

Beijing orders officials to avoid “blind investment” and bubbles in AI

China’s Communist Party Central Committee and State Council issued guidelines on October 9 telling officials to avoid bubbles, blind investment and “swarm-like” rushes into high-tech sectors, and to answer for major losses, Xinhua reported. The guidelines call for AI that is “safe, reliable and controllable,” with risk-warning and emergency-response systems. They also order a full rollout of the “AI Plus” program across electric vehicles, AI phones and PCs, and humanoid robots. No funding figures were given.

Why it matters: Beijing is trying to slow local-government overspending on AI without slowing adoption. Emerging-markets and China PMs should expect tighter scrutiny of provincial AI and robotics funding, which could cool the most crowded names, while AI-enabled consumer hardware and robotics makers stand to benefit from state demand under the AI Plus rollout over the next year.
AI in Wealth Management
eToro (press release)

eToro launches MCP connectors that let Claude, ChatGPT and Grok trade client accounts

eToro released connectors on October 8 that link client accounts to Claude, ChatGPT and Grok through Model Context Protocol, with no API keys or code. Clients choose read-only access or, where available, read-and-act access; every proposed trade needs their approval, and funding and withdrawals stay inside eToro. The connectors are not offered in the US, Australia or Singapore. eToro’s survey of 11,000 retail investors in 13 countries found 56% use or are open to AI tools for picking investments.

Why it matters: A listed broker now lets outside assistants act on accounts, not just read them, a step beyond Robinhood’s in-app agents. US firms should decide which accounts and actions they would expose through MCP, and how approvals and agent-placed orders would be supervised and recorded, before clients ask why their broker cannot do the same.
Ridgeline (company news)

Ridgeline says all live clients now use its AI as it adds agentic workflows

Ridgeline said on October 8 that 100% of its live customers use AI inside its investment-management platform, CEO Dave Blair told its Base Camp conference. The expanded Ridgeline Assistant analyzes files and data across the platform in chat, and new agentic workflows produce client and portfolio status reports delivered in the platform or by email. Ridgeline says its AI system is certified to ISO/IEC 42001. It has $760 billion of assets committed and raised a $250 million Series E in September.

Why it matters: A full adoption claim from a portfolio-accounting vendor raises the bar for Orion, Addepar and SS&C in RFPs. Firms reviewing their core platform should ask each vendor for usage figures, not feature lists, and treat ISO 42001 certification as a due-diligence question alongside SOC 2 when AI touches client and portfolio data.
WealthManagement.com

Orion starts charging partner vendors up to $20,000 a month for data access

Orion is introducing a Connected Ecosystem Support Fee for vendors that pull data from its platform, reportedly $2,000 to $20,000 a month, WealthManagement.com reported on October 8. First invoices go out later in October, and Orion moves to a support fee plus usage-based pricing in the first quarter of 2027. Orion said the fee funds the security and scale of its AI and data work, including MCP access for integration partners. Orion cites $6.6 trillion in assets under administration.

Why it matters: Data access is becoming a paid toll as AI agents multiply the calls on platform data. Orion firms should ask CRM, planning and AI note-taking vendors whether they will pass the fee through, budget for higher integration costs in 2027, and check whether contracts let vendors raise prices for platform charges.
AI in Asset Management
InvestmentNews

Deloitte: 77% of fund groups expand AI in valuation, but 7% keep audit trails

Deloitte’s 24th Fair Valuation Pricing Survey of 95 registered fund groups, released October 8, found 77% began or increased AI use in the past year, up from 35% in 2025. Among users, 73% use Microsoft Copilot, 40% ChatGPT and 29% Claude; 58% use AI to draft valuation memos. Governance lags: 39% have no formal AI governance, and only 7% of valuation users keep output logs or audit trails. A third of fund groups now hold private credit, up from 21% in 2023.

Why it matters: Valuation memos drafted by AI without logs are an exam finding waiting to happen, especially for hard-to-price private credit. Fund boards and CCOs should require output logging, human sign-off and vendor due diligence for any AI used in fair-value work, and ask sub-advisers and pricing vendors to document how they govern it before year-end audits.
Hedgeweek

Lone Pine’s long-short fund lost about 31% in the third quarter on AI stocks

Lone Pine Capital’s long-short fund fell about 31% in the third quarter, including about 25% in July, erasing its gains and leaving it roughly flat for the year, Bloomberg reported on October 9. Its long-only fund fell about 23% in the quarter. The $18–19 billion firm’s largest US long was AI cloud provider Nebius, about $1.2 billion, which fell about 15% in the quarter; Applied Materials dropped 29%. Coatue is up about 12% this year.

Why it matters: A swing from a 51% second quarter to a 31% third-quarter loss shows how concentrated AI-infrastructure bets can reverse. Allocators should check overlap in AI neocloud and semiconductor-equipment names across their tiger-cub and growth managers, and advisors with concentrated AI sleeves should revisit position limits and rebalancing triggers before third-quarter earnings season.
Bloomberg

Franklin Templeton’s Johnson favors short-dated Big Tech debt over long AI financing

Franklin Templeton CEO Jenny Johnson told Bloomberg Television at the Milken Institute Asia Summit in Singapore on October 9 that short-term debt of cash-rich technology companies is the sweet spot for AI exposure. She called off-balance-sheet AI financing guaranteed by hyperscalers and suppliers “very complex” and said longer-dated exposure is “a little bit more scary.” She added that AI “has not played out at all” yet in traditional industries’ productivity.

Why it matters: A top-10 US asset manager steering clients toward short maturities signals caution on the wave of long AI data-center and chip financings. Fixed-income PMs should check how much hyperscaler-guaranteed or lease-backed AI paper sits in core and private-credit sleeves, and favor shorter tenors where spreads do not pay for structural complexity.
