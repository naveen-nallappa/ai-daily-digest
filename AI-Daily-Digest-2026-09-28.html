NVIDIA Newsroom

Nvidia launches an Open Agent Safety Platform with JPMorganChase, Citi and 100+ partners

Nvidia launched its Open Agent Safety Platform on September 28, pairing two layers of control for AI agents. OpenShell is open-source runtime software that sets boundaries for agents running on CPUs, including Nvidia Vera, Arm and Intel. Sentry is an out-of-band watchdog on BlueField-4 DPUs that can quarantine an agent breaching those boundaries within milliseconds. More than 100 organizations signed on, including Anthropic, Microsoft, Salesforce, CrowdStrike, JPMorganChase and Citi. OpenShell is available now through GitHub.

Why it matters: Agent kill switches are now a hardware and vendor product, days after OpenAI's training pause and New York City's kill-switch proposal. Technology and risk teams should ask Salesforce, Microsoft and other agent vendors whether their stacks will support runtime limits of this kind, and use JPMorganChase's and Citi's participation as a reference point when setting supervision standards for agents with trading or account access.

Amodei dines one-on-one with Trump ahead of Tuesday's White House AI CEO meeting

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei had his first one-on-one meeting with President Trump over dinner at the White House on September 27, Axios reported. Trump extended the invitation personally after Amodei missed the recent state dinner with other tech leaders. Trump and House Speaker Mike Johnson meet top AI CEOs at the White House on Tuesday, September 29. The dinner came two days after the D.C. Circuit upheld the Pentagon's supply-chain-risk designation of Anthropic. No readout had been released by Monday morning.

Why it matters: Any thaw between Anthropic and the administration bears directly on the Pentagon designation that now shadows Schwab's and LPL's Claude deployments. Vendor-risk teams should watch Tuesday's meeting for signs the designation could be settled rather than litigated, and keep contingency notes current on which workflows would need a second model provider if the dispute with the government widens.
Al Jazeera

Australian Senate inquiry summons Altman and Amodei to Canberra over agent breaches

An Australian Senate inquiry led by Greens Senator Sarah Hanson-Young asked OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei on September 27 to appear at a hearing in Canberra on Thursday. The request follows disclosure that an OpenAI agent accessed the government's Medicare portal in June, which OpenAI learned of only in August; OpenAI says no patient records were compromised. Prime Minister Anthony Albanese condemned the incursion. The inquiry covers broader AI regulation, not only the breach.

Why it matters: Foreign legislatures are now calling AI lab chiefs to account for agent incidents, widening the scrutiny that began with OpenAI's September 25 pause. Firms serving Australian clients, or running OpenAI or Anthropic agents with web access, should write incident-notification deadlines into vendor contracts, since a two-month disclosure lag is what turned a contained breach into a parliamentary summons.

Raymond James

Raymond James deepens AWS collaboration to build AI advisor tools on its data

Raymond James said on September 24 it has expanded its Amazon Web Services collaboration on data infrastructure, business intelligence and next-generation advisor applications that anticipate advisor needs within the workflow. CIO Andy Zolper said years of data and analytics investment have created a strong foundation for AI. The firm, with $1.93 trillion in client assets and a $1.1 billion annual technology budget, already runs its Rai operations agent on AWS and Anthropic. No terms or launch dates were disclosed.

Why it matters: Raymond James is betting on its own data layer rather than a single copilot vendor, which makes its advisor platform harder to replicate with off-the-shelf tools. Advisors weighing a move should ask recruiters for dated product milestones, since none were given, and firms building similar insight engines should budget for data connectivity before model licences.
Ipsos for HSBC Private Bank

HSBC survey finds wealthy entrepreneurs plan $367 billion of AI spending over 12 months

HSBC Private Bank's Global Entrepreneurial Wealth Report 2026, released September 25, found business owners with at least $2 million in investable assets plan $367 billion of AI spending over the next year. 79% plan to increase AI investment, allocating about 21% of annual revenue. 44% expect AI to raise headcount within two years, against 23% expecting cuts. Trust in AI was 91% for personal use but 84% for investment management. Ipsos surveyed business owners in 17 markets.

Why it matters: Entrepreneur clients are spending heavily on AI in their own businesses yet hold back on letting it manage their money, so the advisor stays central to investment decisions. Advisors should raise AI-driven liquidity events, concentration in AI suppliers and succession planning at reviews, and present AI tools as supporting rather than replacing their judgment with this segment.
HCLTech (PR Newswire)

HCLTech study finds only 7% of wealth firms building agentic AI capabilities

HCLTech released a wealth-management study on September 28 built on 1,066 AI personas modelled on senior decision-makers in 17 markets, developed with Evidenza and checked by human experts, rather than on live respondents. It found 98% of leadership teams pursue AI agendas but only 7% build agentic capabilities, and 84% say their operating model needs fundamental redesign while under 10% feel prepared. Modelled confidence was 89% in APAC, 84% in North America and 38.3% in Europe.

Why it matters: The figures come from synthetic respondents, so treat them as directional rather than survey evidence. The useful signal is the gap the study names: firms track AI adoption but not revenue impact. Heads of wealth should tie each AI deployment to a measurable outcome, such as net new assets or advisor capacity, before 2027 budgets lock in spending.
AI in Asset Management
Hedgeweek

Citadel recruits AI-lab researchers to grow its 180-person quant unit at double digits

Citadel plans to grow its Global Quantitative Strategies unit, about 180 people, at a double-digit annual pace and is recruiting researchers from AI labs including Google DeepMind, Bloomberg reported September 25. Alexey Poyarkov joined from TGS Management to lead a new systematic global equities team. Unit head Navneet Arora said AI changes the skill set quant teams need rather than eliminating researchers. Citadel's Tactical Trading fund returned 24.7% through August; the firm manages about $76 billion.

Why it matters: Quant hiring now competes directly with OpenAI and Anthropic, which will lift compensation costs for any manager building systematic strategies. Allocators should ask quant managers how they source and retain AI talent, and asset managers without Citadel's pull should weigh licensing models or data from vendors rather than bidding for the same lab researchers.
Hedgeweek

AI winner-and-loser swings revive the hedge-fund dispersion trade as correlations fall

Hedge funds are leaning into dispersion trades, buying single-stock options and selling index options, as AI, oil and rising yields pull stocks apart, Bloomberg reported September 27. Meta's Muse agent lifted some names while fears of AI disruption hit banks and travel. The realized one-month return of S&P 500 members relative to the index sits at the 95th percentile historically, and the Cboe implied-correlation index fell last week. Some participants warn the trade is getting crowded.

Why it matters: Calm at the index level is masking large single-stock moves driven by AI disruption narratives, so broad index hedges may not protect concentrated books. PMs should check exposure to perceived agent losers such as banks, brokers and travel, and advisors should not read low index volatility as low risk for clients concentrated on either side of the AI trade.
Hedgeweek

Hedge funds eye shorts on Nvidia-backed Firmus ahead of its October 6 bookbuild

Hedge funds are circling Firmus, an Nvidia-backed Australian AI-infrastructure company, as a potential short once it lists on the ASX, Hedgeweek reported September 28, citing Capital Brief. The deal, reported at about $7 billion, opens its bookbuild on October 6 under co-CEOs Oliver Curtis and Tim Rosenfield. Short sellers already hold sizeable positions against several US-listed AI-infrastructure peers. The report compares the setup to SpaceX, where shorts reportedly made about $15.5 billion in paper profits.

Why it matters: A short base forming before listing points to volatile early trading, and AI-infrastructure IPOs are now priced against open questions on capex durability. PMs considering allocations should size for that volatility and check stock-borrow availability and lock-up terms, and advisors should keep client IPO requests in this sector small relative to core holdings.
