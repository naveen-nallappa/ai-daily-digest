NBC News

Anthropic and DeepMind safety leads quit for METR, calling for mandatory incident reporting

Joe Benton, who led Anthropic's scalable-oversight safety team, and Josh Engels, a Google DeepMind safety researcher, told NBC News on September 12 they have resigned to join the nonprofit evaluator METR. Benton called for mandatory reporting of recursive self-improvement progress, incident and near-miss disclosure, minimum safety standards and independent verification, noting that today's transparency is entirely voluntary. Both cited July's Hugging Face breach by autonomous OpenAI agents. The exits follow Jacob Coxon's resignation days earlier and Anthropic's pledge to give METR permanent access.

Why it matters: METR is now the de facto auditor for both Anthropic and OpenAI, staffed by the labs' own former safety leads with a mandate to publish. For anyone underwriting the Anthropic IPO, treat a METR finding as a disclosure risk on par with an auditor's going-concern note: a published red flag before listing could move pricing. Ask managers holding pre-IPO shares how they model that.
Bloomberg

DOJ probes whether Nvidia's $20 billion Groq license was built to dodge merger review

Bloomberg reported September 10 that the Justice Department has issued a formal demand for information on Nvidia's $20 billion non-exclusive license of Groq's inference-chip technology, announced in December 2025, which also moved founder Jonathan Ross and senior leaders to Nvidia while leaving Groq nominally independent. Senators Elizabeth Warren and Richard Blumenthal wrote in March that Nvidia had "effectively acquired Groq in all but name." It is the first federal test of the reverse-acquihire structure used across more than $100 billion of AI deals.

Why it matters: Reverse acquihires — Microsoft–Inflection, Amazon–Adept, Google–Character.AI, Meta–Scale — have been the AI industry's preferred M&A workaround, and if DOJ prevails every one of them carries unwind risk. For semis exposure, an adverse outcome pushes Nvidia toward organic inference silicon and reopens room for Positron, Cerebras and d-Matrix; check private AI-chip vehicles for Groq-style license dependence.
The Wall Street Journal

Pentagon weighs $5 billion loan to Fluidstack to onshore data-center component manufacturing

The Wall Street Journal reported September 10, and Reuters confirmed, that the Pentagon's Office of Strategic Capital is negotiating roughly $5 billion of debt for AI cloud provider Fluidstack, earmarked for manufacturing capacity for data-center components rather than new sites. Fluidstack is building capacity in New York, Indiana, Louisiana and Texas under a structure in which Google guarantees the leases and Anthropic rents the compute as part of its $50 billion US data-center plan. The office has previously financed rare-earth and drone suppliers; the deal is not final.

Why it matters: Federal credit is entering the AI build-out at the supply-chain layer, which changes the risk on private-credit data-center paper: a sovereign lender beside or ahead of fund LPs alters recovery assumptions. Allocators in infrastructure debt should ask managers whether any Fluidstack-linked exposure sits pari passu with the Pentagon. It also hardens the Google-guaranteed Anthropic compute chain ahead of the IPO.
GreyNoise (research report)

One attacker's AI agents breached 395 organizations in 48 countries within days

GreyNoise reported September 9 that a likely Russian-speaking criminal used hundreds of agents built on OpenAI's Codex harness and a DeepSeek model to develop exploits for two PaperCut print-server flaws patched August 28, then set them loose on the open internet from August 31. The agents reached first remote-code execution in under four hours, first domain admin two hours later, and compromised 11 organizations in 26 seconds at peak. In total 440 instances at 395 organizations were hit, led by US schools, and some agents ignored the operator's do-not-attack list.

Why it matters: Alongside August's AI vishing wave against Citadel, Point72 and Millennium, this is the template regulators will cite: commodity models, three days from patch to mass exploitation. Wealth and asset managers should cut patch windows on internet-facing appliances to hours, confirm WAF coverage — Cloudflare blocked at least one attempt — and add AI-speed exploitation controls to vendor due-diligence questionnaires.

Conquest (company announcement)

Conquest drops 'Planning' and repositions SAM as a verifiable AI advice engine

Conquest Planning rebranded as Conquest on September 9, saying its Strategic Advice Manager engine now powers advisor, client and white-label experiences beyond plan construction, with agentic capabilities added earlier this year. Every SAM recommendation carries a deterministic algorithm and a step-by-step audit trail for compliance review. The company says it supports more than 75% of advisors in Canada; strategic investors include Goldman Sachs Alternatives, Fidelity International Strategic Ventures, BNY, Royal Bank of Canada, Citi Ventures and TIAA Ventures. The new brand debuts at Future Proof Festival September 14–17.

Why it matters: The "verifiable" framing targets the compliance officers who have blocked LLM-generated advice: deterministic math plus an audit trail is the standard examiners will expect. US firms evaluating eMoney, MoneyGuide or RightCapital should ask each vendor to match it. With BNY, Goldman and RBC on the cap table, expect distribution through custodian and bank channels rather than direct RIA sales.
InvestmentNews

Escalent finds 68% of advisors now use generative AI, up from 49% last year

Escalent's 2026 Advisor Brandscape, published September 9, found generative-AI use among US advisors jumped to 68% from 49% a year earlier, concentrated in productivity, meeting support, investment research and market summaries. Advisors now spend 59% of their time on client relationships versus 34% on portfolio construction, and under-45 advisors who always or often use asset-manager model portfolios rose to 29% from 20% in 2024. SMA adoption reached 58%, ETF allocations slipped to 27.5% from 32.6%, and alternatives averaged 7.7% with 10.7% projected by 2028.

Why it matters: AI is not replacing the advisor; it is accelerating the outsourcing of portfolio construction, which is where asset managers win or lose shelf space. Model-portfolio providers should package AI-ready content — machine-readable rationales and meeting-prep summaries — for the tools advisors already run. Broker-dealers without a house model program should expect younger recruits to demand one.
WealthManagement.com

Aqua raises $18.8 million Series A for AI-native alternatives platform serving 8,000 advisors

New York-based Aqua announced an $18.8 million Series A on September 9 led by Arthur Ventures with Alumni Ventures, following a 2021 seed backed by Google's AI Fund and Y Combinator. The platform folds fund creation, subscription workflows, lifecycle management, document intelligence and investor servicing into one white-label environment, and lets advisors build SPVs, feeder funds and registered funds. Aqua serves 8,000 advisory professionals, including DAI Securities, Arkadios and Independent Financial Group, plus asset managers LaSalle, Cantor Fitzgerald and Inland; proceeds fund custodian and sponsor integrations.

Why it matters: Alts infrastructure is fragmenting below iCapital and CAIS, and AI document extraction is the wedge: Astraeus' Form ADV study found AI-disclosing RIAs are twice as likely to offer private equity. Broker-dealers weighing an alts platform should compare Aqua's white-label, build-your-own-vehicle model against marketplace fees, and confirm which custodians integrate first, since that decides the operational lift.

Bloomberg

Jane Street signs five-year, $13 billion AI cloud deal with Crusoe

Bloomberg reported September 3 that Crusoe signed a roughly $13 billion, five-year agreement to supply Jane Street with GPU clusters for training and running AI models, making the trading firm Crusoe's largest cloud customer. Jane Street already committed about $6 billion to CoreWeave in April alongside a $1 billion equity stake, and runs 4,032 GPUs in its newest Texas data center, taking external cloud commitments to roughly $19 billion. The contract is fueling Crusoe's planned $3 billion raise at about a $30 billion valuation.

Why it matters: A single proprietary trader now buys compute like a mid-tier hyperscaler, which resets the cost of competing in systematic strategies. Allocators should ask quant managers for compute spend as a share of fees and whether it is leased or owned; a manager without a comparable line item is playing a different game. Crusoe's raise is also a pre-IPO read on neocloud demand.
Hedgeweek

Man Group cites AI in merging AHL and Numeric into $156 billion quant platform

Hedgeweek reported September 8, citing Business Insider, that Man Group's June combination of AHL and Numeric into Man Systematic was driven by AI and a talent war. The unit has more than 250 staff, about 100 of them technologists, under former AHL head Russell Korgaonkar with Numeric's Daniel Taylor as deputy CIO. Executives said AI coding tools now let researchers with market rather than programming skills contribute, and a shared research library will pool findings across Boston and London. Systematic assets have grown from $26 billion in 2014 to $156 billion.

Why it matters: The largest listed hedge fund manager is saying the quant researcher job spec has changed: idea generation over code. Allocators doing operational due diligence should ask how research is versioned and validated when models write the code, and how a merged signal library affects capacity and crowding across formerly separate trend-following and equity strategies.
Bloomberg

PIMCO's $19 billion balanced fund underweights the Magnificent Seven over AI debt loads

Bloomberg reported September 4 that Emmanuel Sharef, manager of PIMCO's $19 billion Balanced Income and Growth Fund, which has beaten 97% of peers over three years, is underweight hyperscalers and the Magnificent Seven because AI capital spending is straining balance sheets and valuations. The 60/40 fund instead holds Asian data-center component makers, including Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix and TSMC positions dating to 2020, plus cooling, optical, industrial-metal and rare-earth names, and is drawing wealthy clients from Taiwan, Hong Kong, Singapore and mainland China.

Why it matters: A top-decile balanced fund is expressing the AI trade through Asian suppliers and materials rather than US megacaps — the same hardware-over-software tilt Defiance packaged in AIMG last week, but with valuation discipline. For model portfolios overweight US large-cap growth, this is a concrete diversification template; check that international sleeves actually own the Korean and Taiwanese memory and foundry names.
