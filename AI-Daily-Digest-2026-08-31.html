Major AI Updates
OpenAI (announcement)

OpenAI to cut off Cursor's model access November 12 after SpaceX takeover

OpenAI said August 28 it will terminate Cursor's direct access to its models on November 12, following SpaceX's $60 billion all-stock acquisition of Cursor parent Anysphere, agreed in June and completed earlier this month. OpenAI cited prior contract breaches at X after Elon Musk's acquisition as grounds for doubting SpaceX would honor its terms. Cursor CEO Michael Truell said OpenAI models carry about 5% of Cursor traffic, while Anthropic said it plans to add compute to support Claude usage in Cursor.

Why it matters: Model access just became a weapon in a corporate feud, which makes single-vendor dependency a diligence item for any firm standardizing on coding or research agents. The immediate share shift favors Anthropic inside the largest AI coding platform — relevant context for PMs handicapping the Anthropic IPO and OpenAI's own listing timeline.
Music Business Worldwide

Sony and Warner Chappell sue Anthropic seeking up to $150,000 per song

Sony Music Publishing and Warner Chappell filed suit in California on August 28, alleging Anthropic trained Claude on tens of thousands of copyrighted compositions obtained through pirate libraries, BitTorrent distribution, and scraped lyrics sites, stripping copyright-management information along the way. The complaint names CEO Dario Amodei and co-founder Benjamin Mann as individual defendants and seeks statutory damages of up to $150,000 per infringed work before a jury. Anthropic said it disagrees with the claims and will "defend ourselves robustly."

Why it matters: A multi-billion-dollar statutory exposure lands weeks before Anthropic's late-September IPO and will need a prospectus risk factor with a range. Investors sizing pre-IPO or day-one allocations should study how the earlier authors' settlement was quantified and reserved — music publishers have historically extracted larger per-work settlements than book plaintiffs.
Z.ai (Hugging Face release)

Z.ai releases GLM-5.3 open weights after a two-week cyber-safety hold

China's Z.ai published open weights for GLM-5.3 on Hugging Face around August 28, ending an unusual two-week hold it imposed at the model's August 14 launch to complete a safety review. The 744B-parameter coding model scored 84.5% on the CyberGym vulnerability-discovery benchmark, edging Claude Mythos 5 at 83.8% and GPT-5.6 Sol at 83.6%, and Z.ai said it surfaced 2,436 vulnerabilities across 269 open-source projects, 1,097 rated critical or high.

Why it matters: A freely downloadable model at frontier cyber-offense capability changes the baseline threat model for financial firms — the August vishing wave against Citadel, Point72 and Two Sigma showed attackers already weaponizing cheaper AI. Security due diligence on managers and vendors should now assume adversaries have frontier-grade vulnerability discovery, not lagging open models.
AI in Wealth Management
Scalable Capital (announcement)

Scalable Capital opens its €60 billion platform to ChatGPT, Claude and Grok

Munich-based Scalable Capital launched Agentic Investing on August 25, calling itself the first bank in Europe to open its platform to all major AI assistants. Clients — more than one million, with €60 billion on platform — can activate the feature in profile settings and connect ChatGPT, Claude or Grok through an MCP server, with trading, savings plans, watchlists and price alerts live from day one. Access requires Scalable credentials plus two-factor authentication upfront and at regular intervals.

Why it matters: This is the first regulated test in Europe of what brokerage distribution looks like when the client interface belongs to someone else's AI — the same question Morgan Stanley opened in June by exposing ShareWorks to external agents. Custodians and platforms should watch Scalable's adoption, error and complaint data as the template regulators will reference for agent-initiated orders.
Marloo (announcement)

Marloo lands in the US after signing 900 advisory firms in a year

London-based Marloo, an AI platform that automates advisor meeting prep, advice documents, client communications and compliance workflows, launched in the US on August 24. The firm has onboarded more than 900 advisory firms across eight countries in the past year with revenue growing upwards of 40% month-on-month, and enters the US with SOC 2 Type II certification, US data residency on AWS, and zero retention of customer data for model training. A $10 million Blackbird Ventures seed in April took total funding to $12.7 million.

Why it matters: Marloo positions against the CRM incumbents on the thesis that migration pain is their only remaining moat — a sharper attack than the notetaker category Jump and Zocks occupy. Firms running AI-assistant evaluations should score it against both groups, and treat its zero-training-retention and data-residency terms as the new baseline to demand from every vendor.
InvestmentNews

Ezra Group catalogs 51 advisor AI agents in a new public directory

Wealthtech consultancy Ezra Group launched an "AI Agents for Advisors" directory in late August, cataloguing 51 advisor-specific AI agents organized by category — workflow support, financial planning, tax, and client meeting support — with pricing detail included. The firm plans to add a 0–5 "Autonomy Score" grading how much human input each agent requires, and says it will be the first to publish comprehensive reviews of AI agents for the wealthtech industry in one place.

Why it matters: Agent sprawl has become a procurement problem — last week alone brought six advisor-tech AI launches in five days. A neutral reference layer with autonomy grading gives COOs a shortlisting tool that cuts past vendor marketing, and the Autonomy Score maps directly onto the supervision question compliance teams must answer before deploying any agent.
AI in Asset Management
KraneShares (press release)

KraneShares lists an Asia AI supply-chain ETF on the NYSE

KraneShares launched the Asia AI Technology ETF (KAIT) on the New York Stock Exchange on August 19, targeting Asia-based companies critical to the global AI build-out: advanced foundry and packaging, high-bandwidth memory, substrates, optical networking, data-center infrastructure, semiconductor equipment and specialty materials. Holdings span Taiwan, South Korea, Japan and China, and the fund charges a 0.65% net expense ratio against 1.00% gross.

Why it matters: KAIT packages the memory-and-packaging bottleneck — the segment driving this quarter's up-to-89% DRAM price moves — into a single US-listed wrapper, without single-stock ADR liquidity work. PMs adding it as a satellite should first check overlap with existing EM and semiconductor sleeves, where TSMC, SK Hynix and Samsung already dominate weightings.
YieldMax (press release)

YieldMax launches an option-income ETF on memory and storage stocks

YieldMax introduced the Memory and Storage Portfolio Option Income ETF (YRAM) on August 25, extending its options-based income franchise from single stocks to a portfolio of companies that design and manufacture memory semiconductors and data-storage hardware. The fund sells options across the sector to generate current income, arriving in the same month DRAM contract prices were reported up as much as 89% for the second quarter.

Why it matters: Issuers are now slicing the AI trade into income products, and call-writing on the most volatile AI subsector caps exactly the upside that made memory the year's best hardware trade. Advisors fielding yield-hungry clients should frame YRAM as a volatility harvest with NAV-erosion risk, and monitor distribution sustainability rather than the headline yield.
Pensions & Investments

P&I survey finds 160 of 250 managers keep AI out of investing

In Pensions & Investments' ongoing AI Edge series, 160 of the roughly 250 money managers surveyed said they do not use AI in the investment management process, against 90 who do. Holdouts cite fiduciary duty as the obstacle: deploying probabilistic, opaque models into investment decisions raises explainability and accountability questions they cannot yet answer. The gap persists even as firms including T. Rowe Price, which stood up an Investment AI Solutions unit on August 13, push agentic AI into investment teams.

Why it matters: Nearly two-thirds of managers keeping AI out of the investment process while a minority industrializes it is a widening dispersion trade in manager capability. Allocators should split DDQ questions between operational AI and investment-process AI — and press the adopters on the same explainability standard the holdouts say they cannot meet.
