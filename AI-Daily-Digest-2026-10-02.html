Major AI Updates
Sen. Chris Murphy

Hawley and Murphy propose criminal liability for companies whose AI agents hack

Senators Josh Hawley and Chris Murphy introduced the bipartisan AI Agent Accountability Act on October 1. Under the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act, operators would face criminal and civil liability when an agent they knowingly run recklessly causes hacking damage, and developers would be liable for skipping reasonable safeguards against hacking abilities they knew of. State attorneys general could sue for injunctions. The same day, Reuters reported that OpenAI had alerted more than 100 organizations to rogue agent activity and that California’s attorney general had subpoenaed it.

Why it matters: The bill moves agent risk from voluntary standards to personal liability, the opposite of the White House’s self-policing accord. Firms running agents that browse, log in or move data should map each agent’s permissions, keep action logs and check how vendor contracts allocate hacking-law exposure. Compliance teams should ask lab and advisor-tech vendors which hacking safeguards they test before release.
Anthropic

Barclays expands Claude bank-wide, targeting half its developers on Claude Code by year-end

Barclays and Anthropic said on October 1 that the bank is widening its use of Claude. The Claude-based Colleague Knowledge Assistant, live since 2025, now serves more than 16,000 staff and has handled over 1 million searches. In Global Markets, Claude classifies and routes about 120,000 emails a day. Barclays targets half of its developers using Claude Code by the end of 2026 and a majority of software engineers in 2027, including for legacy-system modernization.

Why it matters: Barclays now offers a disclosed benchmark for a universal bank: a knowledge assistant at scale, an operations workload with a daily volume and a dated coding-adoption target. Technology heads at wealth and asset managers can borrow these figures for their own business cases, and should ask how Barclays measures routing errors before copying the approach for client-service inboxes.
Financial Times

Amazon explores moving $8 billion of Nvidia chips into an investor-funded leaseback vehicle

Amazon is in talks to sell about $8 billion of Nvidia Grace Blackwell chips to a special-purpose vehicle and lease them back, the Financial Times reported on October 2. The chips sit in more than a dozen US data centers across five states, including Nevada and Virginia. Outside investors would be offered up to 10% of the equity, with the rest funded by debt. Amazon expects about $220 billion of capital spending this year and declined to comment.

Why it matters: Even the strongest hyperscaler balance sheet is now moving AI hardware off-book, creating investment-grade paper backed by chips that newer generations may supersede within about five years. Credit PMs should price that depreciation risk, not just Amazon’s rating. Advisors with clients in private-credit or infrastructure funds should ask managers how much GPU-backed exposure they hold.
AI in Wealth Management
Orion (Business Wire)

Orion opens MCP access to its 500-plus integration partners’ AI tools

Orion announced the next phase of its integration ecosystem on October 1, backed by its largest R&D investment to date. Over the coming weeks it will extend Model Context Protocol access, the standard for connecting AI agents to software, across Orion Connect and Redtail CRM to more than 500 integration providers. It adds an API sandbox, certification, published rate limits and support commitments. Orion launched its first MCP connector, for Claude, on September 14, and supports $6.6 trillion in assets under administration.

Why it matters: Orion serves 18 of the top 20 Barron’s RIAs, so opening its data to approved AI tools lowers switching costs for notetakers and copilots. Operations leads should list which vendors will request MCP access, set data-scope rules before connections go live, and ask Orion how its audit logs record agent queries against client records.
FPA

FPA relaunches PlannerSearch with profiles built to be cited by AI search engines

The Financial Planning Association relaunched PlannerSearch, its consumer directory of CFP professionals, on October 1. A 12-question assessment replaces ZIP code and asset filters, placing prospects on a “Mindset Matrix” of financial well-being and readiness to engage. Every public profile is structured to be read, indexed and cited by major AI engines. About 4,800 profiles migrated. Listings are a member benefit, with optional paid lead nurturing from Snappy Kraken; The Smyth Group built the platform.

Why it matters: Prospects increasingly ask chatbots to recommend a planner, so being citable by AI engines is becoming a lead source in its own right. Advisors should update their FPA profiles and firm bios with specific specialties and fee details, and marketing and compliance teams should review how AI tools summarize their firm before prospects see it.
RBC Wealth Management

RBC survey finds 85% of North American family offices rank AI their top investment

RBC Wealth Management and Campden Wealth published their 2026 North America Family Office Report on September 29, covering 155 single- and multi-family offices, 123 of them in the US. 85% chose AI as their top investment theme for the next 12 months, and 50% said AI is embedded and essential in their own workflows. Yet 73% still find investment reporting too manual. Cybersecurity was the top operational concern at 59%, up from 16% a year earlier.

Why it matters: Family offices want AI exposure and AI-enabled service at once, while their back offices lag. Advisors serving these families should pair AI-theme allocations with concentration checks and pitch consolidated reporting as an entry point. The jump in cyber concern means any AI tool that touches family data needs a clear security case before it reaches a principal.
AI in Asset Management
SEC (497K)

Global X launches LLMA, an active ETF of about 15 frontier-model developers

Global X launched the Global X LLM ETF (LLMA) on Cboe BZX, with inception on September 30 and an expense ratio of 0.75%. The actively managed fund holds companies building their own large language models that rank among the top 20 developers on benchmark leaderboards and tie at least half of revenue or capex to frontier AI. Up to three anchors can weigh 20% each. Its 12 launch holdings were led by Meta (20.35%), SpaceX (17.41%) and Alphabet (15.64%).

Why it matters: LLMA can add newly listed model developers seven trading days after their IPOs, giving clients a packaged route into this autumn’s lab listings. The top five holdings make up about 76% of the fund, so advisors should check overlap with existing mega-cap tech before adding it and size it as a satellite position, not a core holding.
Carlyle

Carlyle warns private credit’s AI data-center lending risks repeating software’s concentration

Carlyle published research on October 1 warning that private lenders crowding into AI infrastructure debt risk repeating their build-up in software loans, which soured once generative AI threatened those borrowers. Carlyle estimates AI compute may need about $1 trillion of private credit, more than half of the asset class’s current assets. It notes that seven or eight top-tier counterparties make up most of the underlying financings, and that AI-linked assets are more speculative and more tied to the economy than software.

Why it matters: A major private-credit manager is flagging concentration in its own market, days after the Bank of England’s warning. Allocators should ask managers for exposure by counterparty, contract terms and residual asset values, and check whether “diversified” evergreen credit funds sold to wealth clients are concentrated in the same handful of AI borrowers.
Bloomberg

Apollo backs a $15 billion, 400-megawatt AI data center in Chiba, Japan

JERA, Japan’s largest power generator, Dell Technologies and UK developer RHAELM announced on October 1 a $15 billion hyperscale AI data center next to JERA’s Chiba thermal power station. Apollo Global Management will be RHAELM’s strategic investment and financial partner. JERA will supply land and up to 400 megawatts of power for 15 to 25 years. The site opens in phases from 2028 and reaches full operation in 2029, with several gigawatts more planned at JERA sites in the 2030s.

Why it matters: Alternative managers are now financing AI capacity outside the US, pairing long-term power supply with compute in one deal. Infrastructure PMs should compare this power-contract structure with US projects, where grid access is the bottleneck. Advisors placing clients in Apollo or similar infrastructure vehicles should ask how much of the book now carries data-center construction risk.
