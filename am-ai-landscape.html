Scope

This tracks how asset managers use AI in how they work, not what they sell: investment research and idea generation, portfolio construction and signals, trading, risk and compliance, fund operations, distribution and client service, the internal platform that carries it, and whether outside agents can reach the firm’s data. Live tools and announced pilots both count; AI-themed products (AI ETFs, AI infrastructure funds, data-center vehicles) are out of the matrices and appear only in a footer row or the watch list, because they describe a product shelf rather than adoption.

Four buckets, split by the markets a firm manages rather than by whether it is listed. Listing status matters only for evidence: listed managers report earnings, so their cells can reach [P]; private firms mostly sit at [C] and [D].

  1. Public-markets managers — BlackRock, Vanguard, Fidelity, State Street Investment Management, Capital Group, T. Rowe Price, Franklin Templeton, Invesco, PIMCO, AllianceBernstein, Janus Henderson, Nuveen, Dimensional, WisdomTree, with Goldman Sachs Asset Management as the neutral comparator.
  2. Private-markets managers — Blackstone, KKR, Apollo, Carlyle, Ares, Brookfield, TPG, Blue Owl, EQT, Hamilton Lane, StepStone. For this bucket “research and idea generation” reads as deal sourcing and diligence, and a portfolio-company value-creation row is added.
  3. Hedge funds and quants — Citadel, Millennium, Two Sigma, Man Group, AQR, Bridgewater, D.E. Shaw, Renaissance, Point72, Balyasny. This bucket discloses least on the record; expect mostly [D] cells sourced from trade press, hiring pages and conference remarks, flagged as such.
  4. Platforms and vendors — the stack the first three buckets build on: Aladdin, Bloomberg, FactSet, LSEG, MSCI, SS&C, Ridgeline, the AI-native research layer (Hebbia, Rogo, Brightwave, Daloopa, Aiera) and the finance offerings of OpenAI and Anthropic.

Asset owners (public pensions, sovereign funds) are outside the matrices. The companion wealth-management landscape uses the same conventions. A weekday scan can add rows once this framework has been reviewed; until then the page is a point-in-time build dated in the header.

Executive Takeaways

Asset management is a year behind wealth management on disclosed adoption and a year ahead on production alpha. Wealth firms publish advisor-adoption percentages; asset managers publish almost none, yet three hedge funds already run AI-generated signals or AI research in majority use, and the mega-cap alternatives firms are buying portfolio-company AI deployment at billion-dollar scale.

1. The research assistant is table stakes; the adoption number is the differentiator. Every bucket has a firm-wide assistant somewhere, but only Goldman (all ~46,000 staff), Citadel (almost all equities investors), Balyasny (~95% of teams), Carlyle (~90% of staff) and T. Rowe (>70%, unverified) put a number on it, and only Goldman’s and Citadel’s meet the [P] or [C] bar cleanly. BlackRock, Fidelity, Vanguard, Capital Group and PIMCO, five of the six largest managers, disclose no adoption metric at all.

2. Live alpha from AI exists, and it is concentrated in four names. Man’s AlphaGPT has several dozen agent-generated signals in live trading, Bridgewater’s AIA Labs fund is roughly $4.5bn, AQR runs about a fifth of its flagship signals on ML, and Two Sigma’s feature engines compress months of data work into days. No long-only manager claims anything comparable; BlackRock’s Asimov is the closest and is still inside one investment group.

3. Private markets compete on portfolio-company AI, not desk AI. Blackstone, Apollo, Carlyle and EQT each run an in-house data-science team that deploys into portfolio companies, with Apollo’s procurement benchmark across 40+ companies and KKR’s “150-plus companies” as the scale markers. The 2026 shift is to buy this at scale: the Anthropic enterprise-services firm (Blackstone, H&F, Goldman, ~$1.5bn) and the OpenAI Deployment Company (TPG-led, Brookfield $500m) are the two vehicles, and the Google talks would be a third.

4. Agent access is being decided by the data vendors, not the managers. FactSet, LSEG, MSCI and Daloopa have shipped MCP servers; Bloomberg has not; Aladdin has none for institutions. No asset manager in any bucket exposes agent-readable data, and only Man (agentic workflows) and Balyasny (per-team agent frameworks) describe anything internal. The book-of-record analytics layer, FactSet’s Portfolio Analytics MCP in limited release, is where this turns from research into portfolio construction.

5. Two labs, one connector list, different client rosters. OpenAI and Anthropic now offer near-identical financial-data connectors. Anthropic has the named asset-manager clients (T. Rowe, Bridgewater, Millennium, Man, Janus Henderson, Carlyle, Citadel); OpenAI has Balyasny, Morgan Stanley and Evercore as design partners and the larger PE deployment vehicle. Microsoft sits underneath both at Franklin, T. Rowe, LSEG and Carlyle.

Where the gaps are. Trading and execution is empty across all 42 columns; risk and surveillance has one pilot (Millennium). Both are more likely under-disclosed than absent, and both are where a regulator or an LP questionnaire would look first. Blue Owl, Brookfield, Renaissance, D.E. Shaw, Dimensional and Capital Group disclose nothing about internal use.

Implication for a comparator. A public-markets manager benchmarking against this set needs three things to be at the frontier rather than the median: a disclosed adoption metric for its assistant, one research or signal tool that has left pilot and can be named, and a position on agent access to its own data before the vendors decide it by default.

How to read ⓘ
How to read the matrices

The conventions are the wealth landscape’s, with capability rows rewritten for asset management. Every bucket is a matrix of capability rows going down and peers going across; a cell reads “Tool: one line on what it does — Maturity · date or detail [E]”. Click any bucket heading (or its Expand button) to open that bucket full-screen.

Capability groups. Rows are grouped so like-for-like comparison holds across firms: Investment process (Research & idea generation; Portfolio construction & signals; Trading & execution) · Risk & control (Risk, compliance & surveillance) · Operations (Fund, middle & back-office automation) · Distribution (Sales & RFP copilots; Client reporting & servicing) · Platform (Internal LLM platform & data foundation) · External-agent access (MCP, agent-readable data & APIs). Bucket 2 adds Investment process · Portfolio-company value creation. “Firm-wide program / partners” is a footer row, not a capability; disclosed AI spend, headcount and AI-themed products go there.

Maturity scale. Every dated status uses one of five levels: Announced (stated intent, demo or roadmap; no users) → Pilot (limited users, beta or early access) → Limited rollout (in production for a defined subset: a desk, region or client tier) → Live (generally available to the target population; adoption not disclosed) → Scaled adoption (a disclosed adoption or usage metric covering the majority of the target population, or a firm-wide user count). Availability alone never earns Scaled adoption; a cell holding two tools shows both levels.

Evidence tags. Each populated cell ends with a tag for its strongest linked source: P primary-verified (SEC filing, earnings transcript, regulator record, or a firm disclosure corroborated by Reuters, Bloomberg, WSJ, FT or CNBC) · C company-reported (the firm’s own release, newsroom, IR page or a named executive quote) · V vendor-reported (the vendor’s or partner’s release, customer story or self-reported metric) · D directional (a single trade-press or syndicated report, a survey, hiring pages, or unverified). A vendor’s claim about a client firm is [V] even when the client is the column. Anything not confirmed from a primary or trade-press source says “unverified” in the cell.

The pattern is a firm-wide assistant plus one research tool, with distribution the second-most common live use. Only Goldman (firm-wide) and State Street (corporate) disclose adoption above the majority line; T. Rowe's >70% figure is from an unverified call transcript. No firm discloses AI in trading or execution, and no one has shipped agent access. Franklin Templeton and Janus Henderson are the two betting on named external stacks (Microsoft + Wand AI; Anthropic + Percepta).

Maturity scale. Every dated status uses one of five levels: Announced (stated intent, demo or roadmap; no users) → Pilot (limited users, beta or early access) → Limited rollout (in production for a defined subset: a desk, region or client tier) → Live (generally available to the target population; adoption not disclosed) → Scaled adoption (a disclosed adoption or usage metric covering the majority of the target population, or a firm-wide user count). Availability alone never earns Scaled adoption; a cell holding two tools shows both levels.

Evidence tags. Each populated cell ends with a tag for its strongest linked source: P primary-verified (SEC filing, earnings transcript, regulator record, or a firm disclosure corroborated by Reuters, Bloomberg, WSJ, FT or CNBC) · C company-reported (the firm’s own release, newsroom, IR page or a named executive quote) · V vendor-reported (the vendor’s or partner’s release, customer story or self-reported metric) · D directional (a single trade-press or syndicated report, a survey, hiring pages, or unverified). A vendor’s claim about a client firm is [V] even when the client is the column. Anything not confirmed from a primary or trade-press source says “unverified” in the cell.

How to read ⓘ
How to read the matrices

The conventions are the wealth landscape’s, with capability rows rewritten for asset management. Every bucket is a matrix of capability rows going down and peers going across; a cell reads “Tool: one line on what it does — Maturity · date or detail [E]”. Click any bucket heading (or its Expand button) to open that bucket full-screen.

Capability groups. Rows are grouped so like-for-like comparison holds across firms: Investment process (Research & idea generation; Portfolio construction & signals; Trading & execution) · Risk & control (Risk, compliance & surveillance) · Operations (Fund, middle & back-office automation) · Distribution (Sales & RFP copilots; Client reporting & servicing) · Platform (Internal LLM platform & data foundation) · External-agent access (MCP, agent-readable data & APIs). Bucket 2 adds Investment process · Portfolio-company value creation. “Firm-wide program / partners” is a footer row, not a capability; disclosed AI spend, headcount and AI-themed products go there.

Maturity scale. Every dated status uses one of five levels: Announced (stated intent, demo or roadmap; no users) → Pilot (limited users, beta or early access) → Limited rollout (in production for a defined subset: a desk, region or client tier) → Live (generally available to the target population; adoption not disclosed) → Scaled adoption (a disclosed adoption or usage metric covering the majority of the target population, or a firm-wide user count). Availability alone never earns Scaled adoption; a cell holding two tools shows both levels.

Evidence tags. Each populated cell ends with a tag for its strongest linked source: P primary-verified (SEC filing, earnings transcript, regulator record, or a firm disclosure corroborated by Reuters, Bloomberg, WSJ, FT or CNBC) · C company-reported (the firm’s own release, newsroom, IR page or a named executive quote) · V vendor-reported (the vendor’s or partner’s release, customer story or self-reported metric) · D directional (a single trade-press or syndicated report, a survey, hiring pages, or unverified). A vendor’s claim about a client firm is [V] even when the client is the column. Anything not confirmed from a primary or trade-press source says “unverified” in the cell.
