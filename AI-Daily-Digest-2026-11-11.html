CNBC

Anthropic sets IPO range at $70 to $78, valuing it up to $1.4 trillion

Anthropic’s amended filing on November 10 offers 825 million Class A shares at $70 to $78, a raise of $57.75 billion to $64.35 billion, after an earlier version circulated to bankers was withdrawn as inaccurate. The top of the range implies a fully diluted $1.404 trillion valuation, against $965 billion in May. Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley lead the deal, and CNBC reported trading could start on Nasdaq as “ANT” around November 19. Up to 20% of shares are expected to go to retail investors.

Why it matters: A retail tranche of up to 20% means clients will ask advisors for allocations within days. Firms should settle IPO-access eligibility and suitability rules now, and check existing exposure through Capital Group, Fidelity and BlackRock funds before adding more. PMs should plan for index-inclusion demand once the stock trades, since a $1.4 trillion listing would quickly rank among the largest US companies.
CoreWeave Investor Relations

CoreWeave lifts 2026 revenue guidance to $13.0–13.3 billion as shares jump over 20%

CoreWeave reported third-quarter revenue of $3.66 billion on November 10, up from $1.37 billion a year earlier and ahead of the LSEG estimate of $3.55 billion, Reuters reported. It raised full-year guidance to $13.0–13.3 billion from $12.4–13.2 billion. CEO Mike Intrator said its contract backlog more than covers debt obligations, which exceeded $50 billion as of June. The shares rose more than 20% on November 11 after falling over 40% in the prior month, and Nvidia gained about 2%.

Why it matters: CoreWeave is a stress gauge for the AI build-out because it carries heavy debt against contracts with Microsoft, Meta, OpenAI and Anthropic. A beat-and-raise eases fears of near-term order cuts, but its 2030 bonds still yield about 15%. Advisors should separate the equity relief rally from credit risk, which remains high in neocloud debt.
Google

Google adds TPU 8tx and 8i Max, its third and fourth AI chip systems this year

Google unveiled two more eighth-generation TPU systems on November 9. TPU 8tx targets confidential inference with hardware-level protection on AMD EPYC hosts and claims up to 2x lower latency than TPU 8t. TPU 8i Max puts two TPU 8i chips on one package, doubling compute, memory and bandwidth for very long context windows. Google said its TPUs have nearly 100 customers but gave no availability dates or pricing. Bloomberg framed the launch as escalating Google’s rivalry with Nvidia.

Why it matters: Confidential-inference hardware addresses a key objection banks and wealth firms raise about running client data through hosted models. Firms negotiating cloud AI contracts should ask whether workloads can run on confidential-compute chips. For PMs, a fourth TPU system this year adds evidence that hyperscaler custom silicon is taking share of inference spending from Nvidia.
AI in Wealth Management
WealthManagement.com (Bloomberg)

Morgan Stanley’s Jed Finn bets on scale as AI commoditizes advice

Morgan Stanley wealth head Jed Finn told the BMO financials conference on November 10 that AI will make it hard for fintech startups and small RIAs to compete at scale, American Banker reported. He played down the threat from AI agents that move client cash. A day earlier the firm agreed to buy transfer agent Vinyl Equity, expecting $65 million of integration costs over a couple of years. Its workplace unit, which manages cap tables for about half of US unicorns, gathered a record $232 billion of net new assets in the third quarter.

Why it matters: The largest wirehouse is betting that AI favors firms with data, distribution and workplace channels, not small, nimble rivals. Independent advisors competing for equity-comp clients should expect Morgan Stanley to bundle AI-driven planning with stock-plan administration. Smaller firms will need vendor partnerships to match that integration rather than building in-house.
WealthManagement.com

Osaic CEO says AI could move its 9,000 assistants into client work

Osaic CEO Jamie Price said at the firm’s ConnectED conference in Atlanta on November 10 that AI could let its roughly 9,000 registered assistants take on more client-facing work, WealthManagement.com reported. He cited a McKinsey estimate that the industry will be about 100,000 advisors short by 2034. Price said token costs will set how fast Osaic invests in AI. He named no product or launch date, a week after Osaic opened Nitrogen’s Nucleus AI engine to its advisors.

Why it matters: Osaic is framing AI as a staffing model, not a back-office tool: registered assistants backed by AI serving smaller clients. Practices should map which assistant tasks could shift to client service and what supervision that needs. Price’s point on token costs also signals that broker-dealers will ration AI use, so advisors should not assume unlimited access.
WealthManagement.com (Bloomberg)

Vanguard raises about $4 billion of private debt to fund its Altruist purchase

Vanguard raised about $4 billion in private-placement debt to fund its acquisition of advisor platform Altruist, Bloomberg reported on November 10, citing people familiar with the matter. Bank of America, Citigroup and JPMorgan arranged the deal. Vanguard announced the purchase in August after first investing in Altruist in 2020; it oversaw about $13.3 trillion as of July 31. Separately, Schwab Advisor Services’ head of AI, Alison Dooher, told RIA Edge in Austin on November 10 that RIAs should onboard AI agents like new hires.

Why it matters: Borrowing $4 billion shows Vanguard is committed to owning an AI-first custody platform, setting up a direct contest with Schwab for RIAs. Advisors weighing a custodian move should ask both firms for AI roadmaps, data-access terms and pricing commitments in writing. Schwab’s advice to give agents job descriptions and probation periods is a usable supervision template now.
AI in Asset Management
Hedgeweek

Goldman and Capital Group alumni launch Poesis, an AI-run stock-picking fund

Former Capital Group portfolio manager Alex Popa and former Goldman Sachs head of machine learning Charles Elkan launched Poesis, a 10-person Menlo Park firm, Bloomberg reported on November 10. Its AI Clarity strategy has run the founders’ money since August and opens to outside investors in early 2027. It holds 80 to 100 US large caps with about a three-year average holding period, and charges 1% and 20%. AI Clarity lost 0.3% in October, against almost 1% for the S&P 500.

Why it matters: Poesis applies AI to long-horizon fundamental picking, not high-turnover quant trading, which puts it in direct competition with active large-cap managers. Allocators should ask how such funds explain holdings and handle model drift before giving them capital. A three-month record proves little; diligence teams should set track-record minimums for AI-run strategies now.
Reuters (via Lufkin Daily News)

Bond managers turn cautious on AI debt as high-yield spreads hit 348 basis points

Bond investors are growing wary of AI-linked debt as an incoming Democratic Congress prepares data-center rules, Reuters reported on November 10. US high-yield spreads widened to 348 basis points in the November 5 selloff, the most since May 2025, before easing to 342. Galaxy Digital’s Helios data-center bonds fell to 89.25 cents, lifting the yield to 13% from a 9.875% coupon. Capital Group’s Karen Choi said 2027 capex and issuance are largely locked in; Thornburg and Loomis Sayles managers were also quoted.

Why it matters: AI risk is moving from equity portfolios into credit, including high-yield and corporate bond funds that clients see as defensive. Advisors should check how much data-center and neocloud debt sits in their fixed-income sleeves. Managers say the main regulatory risk falls on 2028–2030 projects, so PMs should favor shorter maturities on new AI-linked issues.
Hedgeweek

Morgan Stanley data show hedge funds cut equity risk at the fastest pace in a decade

Global equity hedge funds cut risk in the first week of November at the fastest pace in more than a decade, according to Morgan Stanley prime brokerage data reported by Hedgeweek on November 11. Gross long-short equity positions are down 10.7% since late June. Asia-based managers lost 1.5% on average against a fall of more than 5% in the MSCI Asia-Pacific Index, cutting their year-to-date gain to about 2.6%, against 6% for US managers. US net leverage fell 5 points in one session to 42%.

Why it matters: A second prime broker now confirms what Goldman Sachs data showed last week, so the de-risking is broad, not one desk’s clients. Asia managers held up far better than their benchmarks, which supports keeping regional long-short exposure. Allocators should expect lower gross exposure to mute hedge fund upside if AI stocks rebound, as they did after CoreWeave’s results.
